Note 1

What is a mangrove?

Trees and shrubs that live where land meets sea, rooted in salty tidal mud that would drown most plants. Their tangled, arching roots hold the shore together and shelter young fish, crabs and prawns.

Source: Global Mangrove Alliance, 2021 (opens in a new tab)

Note 2

Two halves of climate action

Two halves

Climate action has two halves. Mitigation cuts the greenhouse gases that cause warming, or draws them back out of the air. Adaptation adjusts to the changes already under way to soften the harm they bring.

Source: IPCC, 2022 (opens in a new tab)

The shield

Mangroves do both. As adaptation, they shield coasts: their tangled roots hold the shore together and take the edge off waves and storm surges before they reach the land.

Source: Global Mangrove Alliance, 2021 (opens in a new tab)

The store

As mitigation, they hold carbon. A hectare can hold up to four times the carbon of most tropical forests, much of it in the deep mud beneath the trees. Cleared, that store escapes: their loss produces up to a tenth of emissions from deforestation, though they cover less than one per cent of tropical forest.

Source: Donato et al., 2011 (opens in a new tab)

Note 3

Lost, and coming back

Over the past half-century, between 30% and 50% of the world’s mangroves have been lost, largely to human activity. The losses are now slowing, and restoration is under way.

Sources: Donato et al., 2011 (opens in a new tab) · Global Mangrove Alliance, 2021 (opens in a new tab)

Note 4

Closer to home

After the wave

When the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami struck, mangroves along northern Peninsular Malaysia helped soften its impact, and replanting along the coast followed. It is slow work: seedlings struggle in soft mud, and some replanting has failed.

Sources: FAO (opens in a new tab) · Hashim & Shahruzzaman, 2016 (opens in a new tab)

Planting on soft ground

Where the shore is eroding fastest, the mud is too soft to hold a seedling upright. So sand-filled geotubes are laid 100 to 150 metres out to sea to calm the waves and leave sheltered mudflats behind for planting. It is patient work: a later survey of Perlis, Kedah and Perak found the mangrove belt still too thin to guard the coast against another tsunami.

Sources: FAO (opens in a new tab) · Hashim & Shahruzzaman, 2016 (opens in a new tab)

Note 1

What is marine litter?

Marine litter is any lasting, man-made solid material that ends up in the sea or on the coast, whether it was dropped, dumped or lost. Most of it is plastic, which is why it lingers: a bottle or a bag doesn’t rot away, it just breaks into smaller and smaller pieces.

Source: UNEP Law and Environment Assistance Platform (opens in a new tab)

In its 2021 global assessment, the UN Environment Programme found that plastics are the largest, most harmful and most persistent part of marine litter, making up at least 85% of it.

Source: UNEP, 2021 (opens in a new tab)

1 of 2

Note 2

Where it goes

Follow one plastic cup. It’s left on a pavement after a run. The next storm washes it into a drain, the drain empties into a river, and the river carries it to the sea. Weeks or years later, the tide brings it back and leaves it on a beach, far from where it was dropped.

Source: Meijer et al., 2021 (opens in a new tab)

Note 3

Two ways to tackle it

Downstream

Clean up what’s already there. Beach cleanups remove litter before it harms wildlife or breaks into microplastics, and they tell us what’s out there. In 2025, more than 8,300 volunteers collected over 28,500kg of rubbish from Malaysia’s coastlines. Drink bottles, cigarette ends and plastic shopping bags topped the list, and 97% of what they found would never break down naturally.

Source: Malay Mail, 2025 (opens in a new tab)

Upstream

Stop it at the source. This means making less waste in the first place, through rules (bans and charges on single-use items), better design (packaging that can be reused or recycled) and everyday habits (carrying your own bottle and bag).
